Equilibration and thermalization

This topic, which originally started as a side-quest, became one of my key fascinations. So what is all the fuss about?

Where is the problem?

Quantum mechanics (and, for that matter, classical mechanics) is time-reversal symmetric. What this means is that if some wave function solves the Schrödinger equation, its time-reversed wave function solves the (conjugated) Schrödinger equation as well. In different words: both forward and backward evolution are physical at the microscopic level.

In our everyday, macroscopic world, however, backward evolution often seems awkward and unphysical. Scrambled eggs do not unscramble, heat does not turn into electricity, and so on. This is the conflict between macroscopic irreversibility and microscopic reversibility, which many people have already thought about.

Why there may not be a problem

From a reductionist point of view, one would of course like to derive irreversibility from microscopic principles. In its strictest form, microscopic reversibility seems at odds with this endeavor. A very powerful perspective on resolving this problem is that macroscopic control entails ignorance. For all practical purposes, in the macroscopic world, we do neither control nor see all microscopic degrees of freedom. Instead, we see a coarse-grained microscopic world. This smearing out of the many (reversible) microscopic degrees of freedom is what makes their evolution seem irreversible.

An analogy: imagine a rod with pendulums of different lengths attached. Each pendulum behaves perfectly time reversal symmetric. Yet, if you look at their entirety through the center of mass for example, the motion looks somewhat chaotic. This is ubiquitous in nature. Coarse-graining many reversible degrees of freedom leads to an emergent seemingly irreversible motion.

What I am working on

There are many approaches to understanding equilibration in quantum systems.

Isolated system equilibration: One standard approach is to understand equilibration as a system-subsystem phenomenon. While the entire system may be isolated, meaning it is not driven and does not interact with any other system, a subsystem is not isolated anymore. Under reasonable assumptions, the subsystem then usually equilibrates. The exact conditions under which this happens are far from obvious and are a subject of study themselves.

Equilibration on average: It turns out, however, when examined through an observable, not only a subsystem, but often the full isolated system equilibrates. Due to the unitarity of the underlying Schrödinger evolution, true equilibration can of course not occur. An initially pure state will remain eternally pure and (for finite dimensional systems) return arbitrarily closely to its initial state. For sufficiently large systems, with the appropriate spectrum, what happens instead is the following: expectation values of observables tend and stay close to a typical value for most of the evolution time. Here, equilibrium is understood in a statistical sense.

Eigenstate thermalization: Thermalization is a stronger concept than equilibration. The latter is simply about a physical system tending towards some equilibrium state, whereas thermalization requires that this state be thermal. One of the most prominent keywords in this context is the eigenstate thermalization hypothesis. In simplified terms, it states that eigenstates of a Hamiltonian look thermal with respect to local observables. As the name suggests, this is a hypothesis. Many systems and Hamiltonians satisfy it heuristically, but rigorously proving this is often quite hard.

  1. PRX Quantum
    Emergence of a second law of thermodynamics in isolated quantum systems
    Florian Meier, Tom Rivlin, Tiago Debarba, and 3 more authors
    PRX Quantum 6 (1), 010309 (2025)
    abs doi arXiv

    The second law of thermodynamics states that the entropy of an isolated system can only increase over time. This appears to conflict with the reversible evolution of isolated quantum systems under the Schrödinger equation, which preserves the von Neumann entropy. Nonetheless, one finds that with respect to many observables, expectation values approach a fixed value – their equilibrium value. This ultimately raises the question: in what sense does the entropy of an isolated quantum system increase over time? For classical systems, one introduces the assumption of a low entropy initial state along with the concept of ignorance about the microscopic details of the physical system, leading to a statistical interpretation of the second law. By considering the observables through which we examine quantum systems, both these assumptions can be incorporated, building upon recent studies of the equilibration on average of observables. While the statistical behavior of observable expectation values is well-established, a quantitative connection to entropy increase has been lacking so far. In deriving novel bounds for the equilibration of observables, and considering the entropy of the system relative to observables, we recover a variant of the second law: the entropy with respect to a given observable tends towards its equilibrium value in the course of the system’s unitary evolution. These results also support recent findings which question the necessity of non-integrability for equilibration in quantum systems. We further illustrate our bounds using numerical results from the paradigmatic example of a quantum Ising model on a chain of spins. There, we observe entropy increasing up to equilibrium values, as well as fluctuations which expose the underlying reversible evolution in accordance with the derived bounds.
